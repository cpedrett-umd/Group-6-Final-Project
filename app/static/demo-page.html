What researchers actually know about memory and aging

By Staff Reporter · 6 min read

Mild forgetfulness is a normal part of aging, and researchers have spent decades separating ordinary changes from the early signs of something more serious. The distinction matters, because the gap between the two is where most confusion — and most marketing — lives.

Sleep, exercise, and social contact remain the interventions with the strongest evidence behind them. None of them are dramatic, and none of them work quickly. That is precisely why they are rarely the subject of advertising.

Supplement marketing frequently borrows the vocabulary of clinical research without the substance of it. Phrases such as "clinically proven" carry no regulatory definition in this context, and the studies invoked are often small, unpublished, or funded by the seller.

Consumer advocates suggest a simple habit: before acting on any health claim in an advertisement, look for the specific study, the specific researcher, and the specific institution. If none of the three can be found, the claim is doing rhetorical work rather than scientific work.

That advice is easier to state than to follow in the moment, which is what makes time pressure such an effective sales device. An offer that expires tonight is an offer that discourages exactly the checking that would undermine it.
